# Tutorial 7 — Minimum Spanning Trees

## Source and Scope
These English notes summarize the supplied Hebrew slides `material/lectures/Tutorial algo 7.pdf` (87 PDF pages), titled "Minimum Spanning Trees — Design and Analysis of Algorithms, Tutorial Seven" (עצים פורשים מינימליים — תכנון וניתוח אלגוריתמים, תרגול שבע). Page references below refer to PDF pages (1–87); from PDF page 29 onward the slide numbers printed on the slides are five higher than the PDF page numbers (PDF page 29 is printed as slide 34). Repeated animation slides are consolidated. The tutorial defines spanning trees and minimum spanning trees, recalls the definition of a cut in a graph, and proves the cycle property and the cut property of minimum spanning trees for weighted graphs in which no two edges have the same weight. It then presents Kruskal's algorithm, with a correctness proof based on the cycle property, and Prim's algorithm, which uses a priority queue; both are animated on the same seven-vertex example graph. The solved exercises check whether a given tree is a minimum spanning tree, find a maximum spanning tree, find a spanning tree whose product of edge weights is minimal, and prove that distinct edge weights make the minimum spanning tree unique. The Union-Find data structure is only named as a way to maintain Kruskal's forest; its operations and analysis are not taught. The slides state no running time for Kruskal's or Prim's algorithm and give no correctness proof for Prim's algorithm.

## Core Concepts
### Spanning Trees — PDF pages 2–4
A spanning tree (עץ פורש) is defined for a connected graph $G=(V,E)$: a spanning tree of $G$ is a tree $T=(V,F)$ where $F \subseteq E$. In other words, it is a tree spread over the graph, made of the same vertices as $G$ and a subset of its edges, so it keeps every vertex and may leave out edges. The slides illustrate the definition on a graph with nine vertices labelled a to i, drawn without edge weights; the builds on PDF pages 3–4 change only the drawing (see PDF pages 2–4; diagram not fully extractable). The same nine-vertex graph reappears in the reminder about cuts (PDF pages 22–24) and on the first slide of the uniqueness exercise (PDF page 84).

### Minimum Spanning Trees — PDF pages 5–7
A minimum spanning tree (עץ פורש מינימלי), abbreviated MST in the exercises, is defined for a connected graph $G=(V,E)$ with a weight function $w$ on the edges: a minimum spanning tree $T$ of $G$ is a spanning tree of $G$ whose sum of edge weights is minimal. The example is a weighted graph with ten vertices labelled a to j and edge weight labels between 1 and 24. The builds on PDF pages 6–7 change only the drawing; since PDF page 15 returns to "the example from before" and asks whether "this" is a minimum spanning tree, they apparently mark a candidate tree (see PDF pages 5–7; diagram not fully extractable). The properties proved next speak of "the" minimum spanning tree of a graph in which no two edges have the same weight, and the last exercise proves that under this assumption the minimum spanning tree is indeed unique (PDF pages 84–87).

### The Cycle Property — PDF pages 8–14
The cycle property for minimum spanning trees (תכונת המעגל לעצים פורשים מינימליים) is stated for a weighted graph $G=(V,E)$ in which no two edges have the same weight: for every cycle $C$ in the graph, the heaviest edge of the cycle, $e=(u,v) \in C$, is not part of the minimum spanning tree $T$ of $G$. Its proof (see Theorems and Proofs) assumes a minimum spanning tree that contains $e$ and exchanges $e$ for a lighter edge of the same cycle. In the correctness proof of Kruskal's algorithm the deck restates the property in the form used there: for an edge $e$ on a cycle $C$, if all the other edges of $C$ weigh less than $e$, then $e$ is not in any minimum spanning tree (PDF page 55). The deck applies or cites the property three times: to check whether a tree in the ten-vertex example is minimum (PDF pages 15–20), to justify the edges that Kruskal's algorithm rejects (PDF pages 53–55), and in the hint of the uniqueness exercise (PDF page 84).

### Cuts in a Graph — PDF pages 22–24
A cut in a graph (חתך בגרף) is recalled as a reminder (תזכורת) before the cut property. Given a connected graph $G=(V,E)$, a subset of edges $F \subseteq E$ is called a cut if there is a subset of vertices $\emptyset \neq S \subset V$ such that every edge $(u,v) \in F$ satisfies $u \in S$ or $v \in S$, but not both, and no edge $e \in E \setminus F$ satisfies this requirement. In other words, $F$ consists of exactly the edges with one endpoint in $S$ and the other in $V \setminus S$, where $S$ is neither empty nor all of $V$. The illustration reuses the nine-vertex graph a–i from the spanning-tree slides, and PDF page 24 shows its vertices in a different arrangement (see PDF pages 22–24; diagram not fully extractable). Both the cycle-property proof and the cut-property proof reason about such a split of the vertices into $S$ and $V \setminus S$.

### The Cut Property — PDF pages 25–28
The cut property for minimum spanning trees (תכונת החתך לעצים פורשים מינימליים) is stated for a weighted graph $G=(V,E)$ in which no two edges have the same weight: for every cut $F$ in the graph, the lightest edge of the cut, $e=(u,v) \in F$, is part of the minimum spanning tree $T$ of $G$. It mirrors the cycle property: the heaviest edge of any cycle is excluded from the minimum spanning tree, while the lightest edge of any cut is included in it. The proof (see Theorems and Proofs) again uses an exchange argument: a minimum spanning tree that avoids $e$ must cross the cut with another, heavier edge on its path from $u$ to $v$, and swapping that edge for $e$ yields a lighter spanning tree. This part of the deck opens with its title slide (PDF page 21) and the reminder about cuts (PDF pages 22–24). Unlike the cycle property, the cut property is not cited again later in the deck.

## Algorithms and Complexity
### Kruskal's Algorithm — PDF pages 29–52
Kruskal's algorithm (האלגוריתם של קרוסקל), presented as an algorithm for finding a minimum spanning tree, maintains at every stage a forest (יער) that spans the original graph $G$. It starts from $n$ trees, each containing a single vertex and no edges at all. It then goes over all the edges of the graph in order of their weights, from the lightest to the heaviest, and adds an edge to the forest if it connects two different trees into one tree. Otherwise the edge is rejected; the correctness proof (PDF page 55) describes a rejected edge as one whose addition would create a cycle.

The forest can be maintained with the Union-Find data structure: each tree is represented by a set, and adding an edge is represented by a union operation on the sets in the data structure. The deck says nothing more about Union-Find, and it states no running time for Kruskal's algorithm.

The algorithm is animated on a weighted graph with seven vertices labelled a to g, whose drawing carries the weight labels 5, 5, 6, 7, 7, 8, 8, 9, 9, 11 and 15; the order in which edges are accepted or rejected appears only in the drawing (see PDF pages 30–52; diagram not fully extractable). The correctness proof follows on PDF pages 53–55 (see Theorems and Proofs).

### Prim's Algorithm — PDF pages 56–74
Prim's algorithm (האלגוריתם של פרים) builds the minimum spanning tree from an arbitrary start vertex with the help of a priority queue (תור עדיפויות). Choose an arbitrary vertex $s$ in the graph and add the edges leaving it to a priority queue. Then, for each edge $(u,v)$ in the priority queue: if one of $u, v$ has not been visited (without loss of generality $u$), mark $u$ as visited, add the edge $(u,v)$ to the minimum spanning tree, and insert into the priority queue all the edges leaving $u$ that have not been visited yet. If both $u$ and $v$ have already been visited, reject the edge. The extracted text does not spell out the priority key; ordering by edge weight, so that the lightest waiting edge is handled first, is implied rather than stated.

The algorithm is animated on the same seven-vertex graph as Kruskal's algorithm, with identical weight labels and the start vertex drawn as $s$ in place of a; the order in which vertices are visited and edges are chosen appears only in the drawing (see PDF pages 57–74; diagram not fully extractable). The slides give neither a correctness proof nor a running time for Prim's algorithm.

## Worked Examples
### Example: Is This a Minimum Spanning Tree? — PDF pages 15–20
Checking whether a given tree is a minimum spanning tree (האם זהו עץ פורש מינימלי?) is the exercise that follows the proof of the cycle property. The slides return to the ten-vertex weighted example of PDF pages 5–7, with weight labels between 1 and 24, and ask whether "this", presumably a tree highlighted in the drawing, is a minimum spanning tree. These slides keep the cycle-property title, so the check applies that property: an edge of the proposed tree that is the heaviest edge on some cycle of the graph cannot belong to the minimum spanning tree, and in that case the proposed tree is not minimum. The builds on PDF pages 16–19 add markings to the drawing, and PDF page 20 shows the example again without them. Which cycle is examined, which edges are compared, and the final verdict are carried by the diagram and are not stated in the extracted text (see PDF pages 15–20; diagram not fully extractable).

### Example: Maximum Spanning Tree — PDF pages 76–78
The maximum spanning tree (עץ פורש מקסימלי) exercise opens the exercises part of the deck (תרגילים, PDF page 75). A maximum spanning tree is a spanning tree of the graph whose sum of edge weights is as large as possible. Given a graph $G=(V,E)$, the task is to describe an algorithm that finds the maximum spanning tree of $G$. The problem slides show the seven-vertex graph of the Kruskal and Prim animations (PDF page 76 with the vertex labelled $s$, PDF page 77 with a).

The presented solution reduces the problem to a minimum spanning tree: define a new graph with a minus sign on all the weights, that is, weight $-w(e)$ on every edge $e$, and find a minimum spanning tree in it. That tree is equivalent to a maximum spanning tree of the original graph, since negating every weight negates the total weight of every spanning tree. The slides do not name a particular algorithm for the minimum spanning tree step.

### Example: Spanning Tree with Minimum Product of Weights — PDF pages 79–83
The minimum-product spanning tree exercise (עץ פורש עם מכפלה מינימלית) gives a graph $G=(V,E)$ with positive weights on the edges and asks for a spanning tree of the graph such that the product of its edge weights is minimal. The deck presents two solutions.

The first solution takes $\log$ of all the weights and finds an MST of the new graph. This is correct because the tree $T$ attains the minimum $\min \sum_{e \in T} \log w(e) = \min \log \prod_{e \in T} w(e)$; the sum and product signs are garbled in the extraction (see PDF page 80; formula not fully extractable). Since the logarithm function is monotonically increasing, this achieves the minimum of the product of the weights.

The second solution is simply to find an MST, with the claim that it is a spanning tree with minimum product. To see this, look at the previous solution, where a logarithm is applied to every weight, and run, for example, Kruskal's algorithm to find an MST of the new graph. Because the logarithm is monotonically increasing, the sorting of the edges is exactly the same, and in general the run is identical. Therefore the MST obtained after the logarithm is exactly the one obtained before it; in other words, there is no need to apply the logarithm at all.

### Example: Uniqueness of the Minimum Spanning Tree — PDF pages 84–87
The uniqueness of the minimum spanning tree exercise (יחודיות של העץ הפורש המינימלי) asks to prove that a graph $G=(V,E)$ in which every edge weight is unique has a single minimum spanning tree. The statement on PDF page 84 literally says "a unique spanning tree", but the title and the proof concern minimum spanning trees. The hint is to assume by contradiction that there are two such trees and to use the cycle property.

The solution assumes two different minimum spanning trees $A$ and $B$. In particular, there is an edge $e_A$ of minimum cost among the edges in $A$ but not in $B$, and another edge $e_B$ of minimum cost among the edges in $B$ but not in $A$. These are two different edges, so by the assumption their weights differ; without loss of generality $w(e_A) < w(e_B)$, so $e_A$ is the cheapest edge that is in one tree and not in the other. Now $B \cup \{e_A\}$ contains a cycle $C$ with $e_A \in C$. Since $A$ is a tree, $C$ has an edge $\tilde{e}$ that is not in $A$, and by the assumption $w(e_A) < w(\tilde{e})$ (the accent over this $e$ is garbled; see PDF page 87; formula not fully extractable). Replacing $\tilde{e}$ by $e_A$ in $B$ gives a spanning tree of smaller weight, a contradiction. ∎ The written solution performs this exchange directly rather than citing the cycle property by name.

## Theorems and Proofs
### Proof of the Cycle Property — PDF pages 9–14
The proof of the cycle property (תכונת המעגל) is by contradiction and uses an exchange argument (טיעון החלפה). Let $e=(u,v)$ be the heaviest edge of a cycle $C$, and assume that some minimum spanning tree $T$ contains $e$. Deleting $e$ splits $T$ into two different connected components. Denote the component containing $u$ by $S$ and the component of $v$ by $V \setminus S$; this is a partition of the vertices of the graph, and the drawing on PDF page 11 marks the two sides.

The cycle $C$ without $e$ is in fact a path from $u$ to $v$. Since $u \in S$ and $v \notin S$, there is an edge $e'=(u',v') \in C \setminus \{e\}$ with $u' \in S$ and $v' \notin S$. Because $e$ is the heaviest edge of $C$, in particular $w(e) > w(e')$. The claim is that $T' = (T \setminus \{e\}) \cup \{e'\}$ is a legal spanning tree that is lighter than $T$. $T'$ is a tree because it has the same number of edges and it is connected, and its weight is smaller because the added edge $e'$ is lighter than the removed edge $e$, contradicting the minimality of $T$. ∎ The accompanying drawing, with vertices labelled $u$, $v$, b, c, d, f and g, illustrates the argument (see PDF pages 9–14; diagram not fully extractable).

### Proof of the Cut Property — PDF pages 25–28
The proof of the cut property (תכונת החתך) is again by contradiction with an exchange argument. Let $e=(u,v)$ be the lightest edge of a cut $F$, and assume that some minimum spanning tree $T$ does not contain $e$. Since $e$ is in the cut, assume without loss of generality that $u \in S$ and $v \notin S$, where $S$ is the vertex set that defines the cut.

$T$ is a spanning tree of $G$, so it must contain a path from $u$ to $v$. Consider the first edge on this path, $e'=(u',v')$, that satisfies $u' \in S$ and $v' \notin S$. This edge is also in the cut, $e' \in F$, so by the assumption $w(e') > w(e)$. By the exchange argument, $T' = (T \setminus \{e'\}) \cup \{e\}$ is a legal spanning tree of $G$ with smaller weight: it is a tree because it has the same number of edges and it is connected, and its weight is smaller because the added edge is lighter than the removed one, so the total weight of $T'$ is smaller than that of $T$. This is a contradiction, since $T$ is then not minimal, and so the claim holds. ∎

### Correctness of Kruskal's Algorithm — PDF pages 53–55
The correctness proof (הוכחת נכונות) of Kruskal's algorithm has two parts. First, the forest obtained at the end of the algorithm spans the original graph $G$; the slides leave the reason as a question ("why?"). Second, the optimality of the algorithm is proved with the cycle property, and for this it suffices to prove that every edge rejected by the algorithm cannot appear in a minimum spanning tree.

An edge $e$ is rejected by the algorithm if adding it would create a cycle. Since the edges are processed according to their weight, the edge that would close the cycle is in particular heavier than all the edges already in the spanning tree, including the other edges of that cycle. By the cycle property, for an edge $e$ on a cycle $C$, if all the other edges of $C$ weigh less than $e$, then $e$ is not in any minimum spanning tree. Therefore the algorithm rejects the edge, as required. A small drawing with four vertices labelled a to d accompanies the proof (see PDF pages 53–55; diagram not fully extractable).
